--[p167]-- When chemical gradients following post-exposure bakes were first compared to the normalized slopes of the aerial images that formed patterns, it was observed that photoacid diffusion generally led to chemical gradients that were less steep than the slopes of the aerial images.<sup>44</sup> This meant that the resist-edge log-slopes (RELSs) were smaller than the image log-slopes (ILSs), and the impact of diffusion has generally been considered as a blurring of the image.

Resist stochastics are also far more significant for EUV lithography, and their impact needs to be taken into account when determining OPC.
--[p168]-- If we consider the photon shot noise to be an effective dose variation, line-edge variations can be reduced by increasing the ILS, because of the well-known increase in exposure latitude with larger image log-slopes.<sup>48</sup> Ensuring that the ILS doesn’t get too small has been done routinely for optical OPC, but the acceptable minimum ILS for EUV lithography needs to be bigger than for optical lithography and weighted more heavily.
--[p168]-- One of the most apparent manifestations of resist stochastics is an increase in the widths of process variability bands (PVbands). In some instances, this increase leads to process failures.<sup>46</sup> This is illustrated in Fig. 6.18, where a traditional PVband calculation, which includes variability over focus, dose, and mask dimensions, indicates good printing over the range of process variations. Inclusion of stochastics shows a failing feature, even at best focus and dose.
--[p167]-- Proper accounting of the diffusion of photoacids and bases is important to achieve accurate OPC in optical lithography, but it is more important in EUV lithography because of the need for greater accuracy (in terms of nanometers) and the fact that concentrations of photoacid generators and photodecomposable bases are higher in EUV resists, relative to optical materials.
As discussed in Chapter 3, alternatives to chemically amplified resists are being considered for use in EUV lithography.
--[p167]-- More recently, detailed modeling of chemically amplified resists that include photo-decomposable bases has revealed more complex behavior. --[p167]-- In this case, the effect of diffusion during the post-exposure bake cannot be described adequately by a simple blur. This behavior is not unique to EUV lithography, but it does add to the complexity of computational problems for EUVL.
--[p168]-- Monte Carlo methods, which can involve hundreds of thousands of iterations,<sup>47</sup> are appropriate for calculating stochastic PVbands for small areas, such as shown in Fig. 6.18, but are impractical for application to fullchip process-window OPC. A first step for addressing the problem of stochastics has been to maintain large image log-slopes when generating the OPC, since a large ILS can help to mitigate LER, particularly the component resulting from photon shot noise.

Whether performing rigorous simulations to achieve accuracy or applying model-based OPC, resist models need to be able to account for the essential physics of the resist in order to obtain useful results. The “resist models” of early OPC engines were small adjustments applied to aerial images to improve the fit to measured data. Over time, such fits have evolved in order to account better for the actual physics of resists.

--[p236]-- 10.4 EUV Multiple Patterning
As with optical lithography, EUV lithography can be extended by means of multiple patterning, based on the same principles. Many techniques, such as methodologies for layout, have already been developed and can be reapplied at smaller dimensions (although improved process control will be needed). On the other hand, EUV multiple patterning is an expensive solution.
--[p236]-- For example, consider the use of multiple patterning for the formation of metal interconnects.<sup>25</sup> Spacer processes are often used for the formation of line/space patterns with very tight pitches without requiring very tight line-toline overlay, but very good overlay is required for the block masks used to form metal line tip-to-tip features and for the masking steps used to eliminate unwanted lines. This can be appreciated by considering the process flow depicted in Fig. 10.13.
--[p236]-- Tight process control requirements have been avoided in optical lithography by the use of self-aligned processes. For example, if the etch process for the “red” material in Fig. 10.13 has a slow etch rate for the “blue” material, and vice versa, the process window can be widened substantially. However, this process would require a minimum of two block masks to work. Thus, using this self-aligned approach requires a minimum of three masking steps.
--[p236]-- With EUV single patterning costs approximately equal to optical triple patterning, the addition of a single EUV exposure is a considerable expense, which can make solutions used for optical multiple patterning impractical for EUV lithography.
--[p236]-- Lines and spaces are formed using a spacer process. Block masks are then used to retain volumes that are not to be filled with metal. However, process tolerances for the blocks are tight. If we consider a line/space pitch of 20 nm, with equal lines and spaces, then control requirements for the gap between the block and the adjacent feature is ±5 nm, which includes dimensional control for the block feature and the lines, plus overlay.
--[p236]-- Multiple patterning for EUV lithography can imply triple or quadruple patterning, rather than double patterning,<sup>25</sup> which will be expensive. However, given the high prices for high-NA exposure tools, it may make sense to use multiple patterning with less expensive, and perhaps depreciated, 0.33-NA exposure tools.

--[p152]-- In addition to the considerations of conventional OPC with tight tolerances, the oblique illumination and the particular structure of EUV masks combine to necessitate consideration of rigorous of models to a much greater degree than for optical lithography in order to calculate aerial images with the necessary accuracy. ===== [p22-22] Extreme Ultraviolet Lithography > 1.4 General Issues (6块) =====
--[p22]-- In this section, some brief mention will be made of important considerations that will be important for all aspects of EUV lithography but that will not be discussed in detail until later chapters.
One principal concern that affects all aspects of EUV technology is cost. There has been an enormous investment made into EUV lithography, and these investments need to be recovered. Moreover, extremely tight process requirements drive up the costs of equipment.
--[p22]-- This makes exposure tool productivity, driven by equipment reliability and throughput, very important.
A key issue that makes it challenging to achieve high throughput is light source power. As just discussed, EUV lithography must necessarily be practiced at wavelengths where there are multilayer films with high reflectance, not where there are strong sources of light.
--[p22]-- The world of EUV lithography is one in which 0.1 nm—an angstrom—is significant.
EUV lithography is practiced only for very-leading-edge integrated circuit processes. As such, all wafer equipment is designed for 300-mm wafers, and throughout this book it will always be assumed that wafers are 300 mm in diameter.
--[p22]-- Something else to keep in mind is that EUV lithography was first used in high-volume manufacturing for the 7-nm logic node, with a \~40-nm minimum metal pitch, and future use will be at even smaller dimensions. Device requirements for such small features drive the levels of performance necessary for EUV lithography. Overlay, critical dimension control, and line-edge roughness (LER) must be very tight, and all aspects must meet these demanding requirements.
--[p22]-- This is in stark contrast to optical lithography, which has always been practiced where there are intense sources of narrow-bandwidth light, such as at the spectral lines of mercury or at the emission wavelengths of excimer lasers. With EUV technology, there never seems to be enough photons.
--[p22]-- 1.4 General Issues
When establishing a lithographic technology, it is essential that all relevant factors meet the requirements for high-volume manufacturing, which means that few factors can be considered in isolation. On the other hand, a book such as this one must necessarily have a linear flow for comprehensibility, so topics relevant to one subject may not be covered in the book until a later chapter.

--[p64]-- Without increasing the lens reduction, the mask 3D effects described in Chapter 6 will become more significant with an increased NA. This consideration affected system architectural choices for high-NA EUV exposure tools, discussed in more detail in Chapter 10.
--[p63]-- Finite angles are needed for mechanical clearances, and the range of angles through the numerical aperture must be accommodated. Reflectivity from surfaces is typically a function of the angle of incidence, and this is particularly true for multilayer mirrors, where reflectance falls off steeply at angles away from where the maximum reflectance occurs (Fig. 3.9).

--[p62]-- In EUV exposure systems, light beams from the condenser optics illuminate a reflective mask that has areas that are either of high or low reflectance, according to the pattern to be printed, much as a conventional mask for optical lithography has areas of high and low transmission. The resulting optical pattern is imaged by a lens comprising several mirrors. Early, experimental low-NA (≤0.1) EUV lenses usually had two or four mirrors, while current 0.33-NA systems have six mirrors.
--[p64]-- All light rays must be incident from the same side of the normal to the mask, and all light rays are collected on the other side of the normal. The chief ray angle is a function of the numerical aperture (at the mask) and increases with a larger NA (assuming a fixed lens reduction). Reducing the angles of incidence nearer to normal requires a lower NA (at the wafer) or larger lens reduction.

--[p121]-- Because EUV lithography is intended to be used to produce features and pitches beyond the resolution limits of ArF lithography, EUV resists must be capable of very high resolution and have low line-edge roughness. ===== [p222-223] Chapter 7 Process Control for EUV Lithography > 9.2 Mask Costs (10块) =====
--[p222]-- To begin, EUV mask blanks are considerably more expensive than optical ones, with low defect blanks that have been qualified with actinic inspection costing \$100k or more.<sup>6</sup> As yields improve, this cost can be expected to decrease, but EUV mask blanks will likely continue to cost more than optical blanks due to the need for expensive ULE glass substrates and very tight flatness requirements.
--[p222]-- 9.2 Mask Costs
The cost of masks has long been an important consideration for lithography, with costs increasing for each new node. EUV masks are also expensive, costing \$250k and more. This cost is driven by a number of factors.
--[p223]-- In a study of mask usage at Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), it was found that an average reticle was used to expose only 1800–2400 wafers.<sup>9,10</sup> From a follow-up survey of SEMATECH member companies, it was found that this level of mask usage was not atypical, although there was a wide range of reticle usage that varied according the type of product being produced. With only a few thousand wafers produced per mask, the contribution of masks to die costs would be considerable.
--[p222]-- Complex film stacks are also deposited on the front-side of EUV masks, which adds to costs even without considering the need for extremely low levels of defects in the multi-layer reflector. Finally, a backside film is needed, unlike the situation that occurs in optical lithography.
The metrology described in Chapter 8 contributes to mask blank costs.
--[p222]-- Defect inspection of patterned masks is a large cost for mask making, even optical ones. When DUV tools are used to inspect EUV masks, the inspection costs are comparable to the costs for optical masks. As noted in Chapter 4, there are benefits to inspecting at EUV wavelengths. Actinic defect inspection tools are more expensive than DUV ones, leading to an increase in inspection costs, although there are benefits in the ability to ensure that masks are defect free.
--[p222]-- Maintaining reflectivity of the multilayer reflector requires measurement at EUV wavelengths, necessitating the use of specialized tools.<sup>7,8</sup> It has also proven necessary to perform defect inspection of the multilayer film stack at EUV wavelengths.
--[p222]-- Because LER can transfer more readily from mask to wafer for EUV lithography, due to the higher resolution of the exposure tools, it is important to minimize mask absorber LER. As discussed in Chapter 4, multibeam mask writers have enabled the use of higher e-beam doses to produce small features with acceptable mask absorber LER. The need to use leading-edge mask writers, which cost on the order of \$50M, contributes to EUV mask costs.
--[p222]-- While DUV inspection tools provide reasonable capability for inspecting patterned EUV masks, AIMS tools (used to confirm the printability of defects and assessing the efficacy of defect repair) must necessarily be actinic, making them more expensive than their DUV counterparts.
The contribution of mask costs is highly dependent on the number of wafers exposed per mask.
--[p222]-- Moreover, in order to enable the pattern-shift methodology to cover remaining multilayer defects with absorbers, as described in Chapter 4, defect inspection tools for EUV blanks need to have capability for alignment to mask fiducials and need to have precise stages. This results in greater metrology costs for the defect inspection of EUV mask blanks than for optical ones, for which defect inspection already contributed significantly to cost.
--[p223]-- Accordingly, EUV lithography is most appropriate for products produced in high volumes. Note that EUV lithography can have advantages when single EUV exposures are replacing three or more optical exposures.

===== [p218-219] Chapter 7 Process Control for EUV Lithography > 9.1.2 Maintenance costs (6块) =====
--[p218]-- Fortunately, most collector mirrors and droplet generators can be refurbished, significantly reducing replacement costs.
Maintenance is often accomplished with service contracts with the equipment supplier, particularly for very complex pieces of equipment with many components unique to the tool. If such service contracts are paid on an annual basis, then the cost per wafer is dependent on system output.
--[p218]-- Such costs will also become less as the technology matures, and there are fewer sub-system designs, as redesigned sub-systems require additional inventories of replacement parts.
Certain components with high failure rates or rapid degradation need periodic replacement, and these are sometimes categorized as consumables.
--[p219]-- For example, assume an annual maintenance contract that costs \$3M per tool, including the cost of replacement components charged to the chip maker. With 100 wph net throughput per tool, and with 80% utilization, the maintenance cost per wafer is \$4.28, or about 10% of the capital costs.
--[p218]-- All in all, this contributes to higher maintenance costs for EUV exposure tools than their optical counterparts, apart from higher downtime.
Broken components need to be replaced, and such parts have direct costs that contribute to the cost of maintenance, as do inventory costs for spare parts. Initially for EUV lithography, inventory costs can be considerable. Inventory costs per tool will decline as more EUV exposure systems are operated.
--[p218]-- 9.1.2 Maintenance costs
As noted above, equipment downtime has a large impact on the contribution of capital to wafer costs, by reducing the amount of material that can be processed during a given period of time. There are also direct maintenance costs. Exposure tool complexity contributes to reliability challenges, but it also means that the people who repair the tools need to be highly trained. Often, sub-system specialists are needed, which leads to a need for more maintenance technicians.
--[p218]-- These are very different in character from other consumables, such as gases and photochemicals, in that maintenance technicians are required for performing replacements, so they are classified as maintenance costs in this book. For EUV exposure systems, components in the light source, particularly collector mirrors and droplet generators, are among those with the shortest lifetimes. These are components that require precision fabrication and are hence costly.

This is clearly a negative resist, so it is not well suited for imaging contacts and vias, but there is considerable interest in using such resists for line and space patterns. For the metal in the core, tin is often used,<sup>49</sup> but zirconium and hafnium have also been considered.<sup>50</sup> Such metals have high optical absorption (Fig. 1.3), which is an advantageous characteristic.
--[p139]-- The incorporation of metals into the resist formulation may also decrease electron scattering mean free paths, thereby reducing image blur by the electrons and also reducing stochastic defects due to the mechanisms described in Section 5.2.
Incorporating metals is helpful for etch selectivity. However, such high selectivity can be a source of defects, since very small amounts of residual resist that does not dissolve in developer can inhibit etch.
--[p139]-- 5.4 Metal-Oxide EUV Resists
Because of the many issues associated with chemically amplified resists, resists based on different types of radiation chemistry are being considered. One class of new resists, based on metal oxides, has received significant attention. Such resists have metal oxide cores with organic ligands attached. Secondary electrons or photons directly convert these ligands to active sites, thereby enabling two adjacent cores to bond chemically (Fig. 5.20).
--[p139]-- Higher levels of defects are seen after etch in metal-oxide resists than post-develop, and this has delayed the use of this class of resists in high-volume manufacturing.
In one study, measurements of overlay were high when metal-oxide resists were used, in comparison to the situation where chemically amplified resists were used.<sup>52</sup> It was determined that grains had formed in the patterned metaloxide resists, which led to noise in the optical overlay measurements.

--[p12]-- First, the patterns on wafers are replicated 1:1 from the mask, so mask quality, in terms of registration, dimensional control and defects, had to be exceedingly good. Second, fundamentally and ultimately more consequential, were the diffraction effects that became insurmountable for printing features with dimensions <100 nm.
--[p11]-- 1.1 Historical Background
For several decades, the resolution capability of optical lithography was extended by a succession of transitions to shorter imaging wavelengths. The first commercially available wafer stepper operated at a visible wavelength (436 nm), while later generations of exposure tools imaged at mid-ultraviolet and deep-ultraviolet wavelengths (Table 1.1).
--[p12]-- Sematech also funded an EUV Micro-Exposure Tool (MET) that was placed at a synchrotron light source at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory.<sup>13,14</sup> The EUV MET provided essential capability for improving EUV resists.<sup>15,16</sup>
Another key event in the development of EUV lithography was the decision by ASML to build a pair of full-field exposure tools, the Alpha Demo Tools (ADTs).
--[p12]-- These were installed at the consortia INVENT in Albany, NY and Imec in Leuven, Belgium, further expanding access to EUV exposure capability. The full-field exposure capability enabled the accomplishment of an important milestone in moving EUV lithography from the laboratory to the factory: the fabrication of functioning SRAMs on a test chip from Advanced Micro Devices with an interconnect layer patterned with EUV lithography.<sup>17</sup>
--[p11]-- During efforts to continue this process of improving resolution by using a wavelength of 157 nm, with $\mathrm { F } _ { 2 }$ excimer lasers as the light sources, a number of difficulties were encountered, and optical lithography was eventually extended not by using light with a wavelength of 157 nm but by adapting ArF lithography to an immersion configuration that enabled the numerical aperture (NA) to be increased to 1.35.
--[p11]-- There were some additional efforts to increase the numerical aperture of ArF immersion lithography further, but development activities were eventually suspended because progress was slow.<sup>1</sup> The shortest wavelength used in optical lithography continues to be 193 nm.
In spite of the lack of success in developing HVM-capable lithographic technologies at 157 nm (or shorter optical wavelengths), lithographers were not ready to discontinue the pursuit of extending lithographic capability.
--[p12]-- However, in the 1980s, multilayer films were developed that provided practical reflectivities at wavelengths 4 nm < l < 25 nm.<sup>2,3</sup> This development led to proposals for lenses with all-reflecting optics that could be used for projection lithography.<sup>4,5,6</sup> Initially, this was referred to as soft x-ray lithography, but the name “extreme ultraviolet” was later adopted to distinguish this new type of projection lithography from proximity 1 x-ray lithography.
--[p12]-- Unfortunately, projection optics at this wavelength could not be fabricated, principally because there are no materials that are sufficiently transparent at l 1 nm to form refractive optics, or have sufficient reflectivity for nearnormal incident light, thus precluding reflective optics. This meant that x-ray lithography involved proximity printing, resulting in two significant problems.
--[p12]-- In addition to co-sponsoring annual EUV lithography symposia, Sematech engaged in development activities directly. A small pilot line was established to produce low-defect EUV mask blanks.<sup>9,10,11,12</sup> The associated metrology capabilities were used to support mask blank fabrication at commercial suppliers.

Very large currents will create strong magnetic fields, which will serve to compress the plasma.
